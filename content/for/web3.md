## Why web3 competitive monitoring is its own problem

Web3 is unusually transparent and unusually fast, and those two traits pull in opposite directions.

Almost everything a competing protocol does is public. The docs are open, the fee schedule is a page, the roadmap is published, governance happens in the open, and launches are announced on-chain and on social. There is more observable competitive signal in web3 than in almost any other market. The catch is that it moves in days, not quarters, and it is scattered across a protocol's site, its docs, its governance forum, its Discord, and its social channels. A competitor can change incentives, ship an integration, or re-price over a weekend.

So the problem is not access, it is keeping up. Checking a dozen rival protocols across five surfaces each, in a market that moves this fast, is not sustainable by hand. This is where automated monitoring of the public surfaces earns its place.

## What to monitor about a competing protocol

Focus on the public surfaces that signal direction and economics: documentation, fee and tokenomics pages, roadmaps, governance forums, and announcement channels.

**Documentation** is the earliest tell. Docs are usually updated when a feature, chain, or integration is close, often before any announcement, so a docs change is a leading signal of what a competitor is about to ship.

**Fee schedules and tokenomics pages** are the web3 equivalent of a pricing page. A change to fees, emissions, or incentives directly affects where liquidity and users flow, and it changes how your project should be positioning.

**Roadmaps and governance posts** show intended direction and what the community is actually voting on, which is often a truer signal than marketing.

**Announcement channels** confirm what shipped. By the time it is here it is public knowledge, so the edge comes from catching the earlier signals first.

## What monitoring public pages does not cover

Being straight about the boundary: monitoring public web pages is not on-chain analytics, and a serious web3 team needs both.

Public-page monitoring tells you what a competitor is saying, documenting, pricing, and planning. It does not tell you TVL, active addresses, token price, or flows. Those come from on-chain analytics platforms, and nothing here replaces them. The two are complementary: on-chain data tells you what is happening to a competitor's numbers, public-page monitoring tells you what they are about to do and how they are framing it. Anyone selling you a single tool that claims to do both well is overselling one of them.
